This page defines the foundational concepts and terminology used across the Capria Ventures knowledge base. Consistent language matters when teams are reviewing fund data, discussing portfolio companies, or communicating with LPs — use the definitions below as your reference point so that everyone is working from the same understanding.

## Fund Concepts

These terms describe how venture capital funds are structured, capitalized, and managed over their lifecycle.

<Accordion title="Fund">
  A fund is a pooled investment vehicle formed to deploy capital into a defined set of investments according to a stated strategy. Each Capria fund has a target size, a defined geographic or sector focus, a vintage year, and a fixed term governing when capital must be deployed and returned to investors.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Vintage Year">
  The vintage year is the calendar year in which a fund made its first investment. It is used as a reference point when benchmarking a fund's performance against peer funds, since funds raised and deployed during the same macro environment are the most comparable. A fund's vintage year is fixed at inception and does not change.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Committed Capital">
  Committed capital is the total amount of capital that limited partners have legally pledged to contribute to a fund over its investment period. It represents the fund's target size once fully subscribed and serves as the denominator for calculating management fees and ownership percentages among LPs.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Called Capital">
  Called capital — also referred to as paid-in capital — is the portion of committed capital that the GP has formally requested from LPs through capital call notices. Capital is typically called in tranches as investment opportunities are identified and fund expenses arise. Called capital is the basis for calculating performance metrics such as MOIC and DPI.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Uncalled Capital (Dry Powder)">
  Uncalled capital, commonly referred to as dry powder, is the portion of committed capital that LPs have pledged but the GP has not yet called. It represents the fund's remaining deployment capacity. Tracking dry powder accurately is essential for portfolio construction planning and LP communications about the fund's pace of investment.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Fund Life">
  Most venture funds are structured with a ten-year term. The first five years constitute the investment period, during which the GP actively sources deals, makes new investments, and deploys committed capital. The final five years form the harvest period, during which the GP focuses on supporting existing portfolio companies, executing exits, and returning capital to LPs. Extensions beyond ten years are possible with LP approval.
</Accordion>

## Portfolio Concepts

These terms describe the companies in which a fund invests and how those investments are structured and managed over time.

<Accordion title="Portfolio Company">
  A portfolio company is any company in which a Capria fund has made an equity investment. Each portfolio company has a dedicated record in the knowledge base containing its investment history, current ownership stake, key contacts, board representation, and monitoring data. Portfolio companies remain active records until the investment is fully exited.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Investment Thesis">
  The investment thesis is the strategic rationale that justifies making an investment in a specific company or sector. It articulates why the opportunity exists, how the company is positioned to capture it, and why now is the right time to invest. A clearly documented thesis is essential for post-investment monitoring and for evaluating whether the original assumptions are playing out as expected.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Follow-on Investment">
  A follow-on investment is a subsequent capital deployment into an existing portfolio company, typically made during a later funding round. Follow-on investments allow the fund to increase its position in high-performing companies, maintain ownership percentage, and support the company's continued growth. Follow-ons are tracked separately from initial investments in each company's record.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Pro-rata Rights">
  Pro-rata rights give the fund the contractual right to participate in a portfolio company's future funding rounds in proportion to its current ownership stake. Exercising pro-rata rights allows the fund to maintain its ownership percentage as the company raises additional capital and the cap table expands. Whether and how to exercise these rights is an active portfolio management decision tracked within each company record.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Reserve Ratio">
  The reserve ratio is the proportion of a fund's total committed capital set aside for follow-on investments in existing portfolio companies rather than new deals. A typical reserve ratio might allocate 40–50% of the fund for follow-ons. Managing reserves carefully is critical to ensure the fund has sufficient capital to support its best-performing companies through to exit without over-concentrating in underperformers.
</Accordion>

## Performance Metrics

These metrics are the standard measures used across the venture capital industry to evaluate and communicate fund and portfolio performance.

<Accordion title="IRR (Internal Rate of Return)">
  IRR is the annualized rate of return that equates the present value of all cash inflows (distributions back to LPs) with all cash outflows (capital calls from LPs), accounting for the precise timing of each cash flow. IRR rewards early exits and penalizes slow deployment. It is the primary time-weighted return metric used in VC reporting and is typically calculated at both the fund level and the individual investment level.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="MOIC (Multiple on Invested Capital)">
  MOIC measures the total value returned by an investment relative to the capital invested, expressed as a multiple (e.g., 3.0x). It is calculated as total value — realized proceeds plus remaining fair market value — divided by the total capital invested. Unlike IRR, MOIC does not account for the time required to generate returns, making it a useful complement to IRR for understanding the magnitude of value creation.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="TVPI (Total Value to Paid-In)">
  TVPI is the sum of DPI and RVPI and represents the total value of the fund — both realized and unrealized — relative to the capital LPs have paid in. A TVPI above 1.0x means the fund has created value above the capital invested. TVPI is the headline multiple most commonly used in LP reports to summarize overall fund performance at any point in the fund's life.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="DPI (Distributions to Paid-In)">
  DPI measures the realized returns that have actually been distributed back to LPs relative to the capital they have paid in. A DPI of 1.0x means LPs have received back exactly what they invested; a DPI above 1.0x means they are in profit on a cash-returned basis. DPI increases as portfolio companies are exited and proceeds are distributed, and it is ultimately the metric that determines whether an LP received a real cash return.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="RVPI (Residual Value to Paid-In)">
  RVPI measures the current fair market value of the unrealized portfolio relative to the capital LPs have paid in. It represents the portion of total fund value that has not yet been realized through exits or distributions. RVPI decreases over the life of the fund as companies are exited and converted into DPI. Early in a fund's life, TVPI is dominated by RVPI; in a mature fund, DPI should be the larger component.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="PME (Public Market Equivalent)">
  PME is a benchmarking methodology that compares a fund's cash-flow-adjusted returns to what an investor would have earned by investing the same capital, on the same dates, into a public market index such as the MSCI Emerging Markets or S&P 500. A PME above 1.0x indicates the fund outperformed the chosen public benchmark on a risk-adjusted basis. PME is particularly important for LPs evaluating whether illiquid VC exposure delivered a premium over liquid alternatives.
</Accordion>

## LP / Investor Concepts

These terms describe the relationship between the fund's investors and its managers, including how economics and governance are structured.

<Accordion title="LP (Limited Partner)">
  A limited partner is an investor who commits capital to the fund but takes no active role in investment decisions or fund management. LPs have limited liability — their maximum loss is capped at their committed capital. LPs receive regular reporting from the GP and have certain governance rights (such as approving extensions or removing the GP for cause), but day-to-day investment authority rests with the GP.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="GP (General Partner)">
  The general partner is the fund manager — in this case, Capria Ventures — responsible for sourcing investments, making allocation decisions, managing portfolio companies, and ultimately returning capital to LPs. The GP has unlimited liability for the fund's obligations and is compensated through management fees and carried interest. The GP's economic alignment with LPs is established through GP co-investment alongside the fund.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Management Fee">
  The management fee is an annual fee charged to LPs to cover the fund's operating expenses, including team compensation, legal and accounting costs, and office expenses. It is typically calculated as a percentage of committed capital (often 2%) during the investment period and may step down to a percentage of invested or net asset value during the harvest period. Management fees are not a profit mechanism — they are intended to cover the cost of running the fund.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Carried Interest (Carry)">
  Carried interest is the GP's share of fund profits above the hurdle rate, typically set at 20% of net profits. Carry is the primary economic incentive for the GP and is only paid after LPs have received back their invested capital plus the preferred return. Carry aligns the GP's interests with LP outcomes — the GP earns significantly only when LPs earn well. Carry may be subject to a clawback provision if early distributions later prove to have been overstated.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Hurdle Rate (Preferred Return)">
  The hurdle rate, also called the preferred return, is the minimum annualized return that LPs must receive on their invested capital before the GP is entitled to any carried interest. It is typically set at 8% per annum. The hurdle ensures that LPs are compensated for the time value and illiquidity of their capital before the GP participates in upside. Some fund structures include a GP catch-up provision above the hurdle before the standard carry split applies.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Waterfall">
  The waterfall is the contractual order in which distributions from fund exits are allocated between LPs and the GP. A typical American waterfall distributes proceeds in the following sequence: (1) return of LP capital, (2) preferred return to LPs at the hurdle rate, (3) GP catch-up to the carry percentage, and (4) remaining profits split between LPs and the GP per the carry arrangement. A European waterfall requires full return of all LP capital across the fund before carry is paid. The waterfall structure is defined in the fund's limited partnership agreement.
</Accordion>
